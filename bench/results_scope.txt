Refinery scheduling and process optimization
============================================

Measured 28 Aug 2026, aarch64 Linux, GCC 11, two cores.

WHY THESE TWO FAMILIES EXIST
----------------------------
PS 26119 names eight application areas.  Every one of them had an instance
family in bench/ except two: refinery SCHEDULING, and PROCESS optimization.
Both were being covered by argument -- "the planning LP is the same shape" --
rather than by an instance anybody could run.  An area with no instance is an
area with no evidence, so here are the instances, and here is what solvers
other than this one say about them.

    python3 -c "import sys; sys.path.insert(0,'bench'); \
        from generate import refinery_schedule; \
        refinery_schedule(0,6,3,12,3,5,4,2).write('sched_m_0.mps')"
    python3 -c "import sys; sys.path.insert(0,'bench'); \
        from generate_qp import process_rto; \
        process_rto(0,14,8,6,3).write('qrto_m_0.mps')"

  Or take the bundled ones: `igaos -m sched_m`, `igaos -m qrto_m`.


1. REFINERY SCHEDULING  (MILP)  --  bench/generate.py: refinery_schedule
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
The scheduling problem that sits UNDER the planning LP.  The planner decides
volumes over a month; the scheduler decides the order and the switches over a
week, and the switches are what make it combinatorial:

  · one operating mode per unit per slot -- max-gasoline, max-distillate, off --
    as a set-partitioning row, so the relaxation splits fractionally across
    modes and has to be branched;
  · a changeover binary that fires when the mode changes, carrying a real cost,
    because swinging a cat cracker is hours of off-spec product;
  · a minimum run length, so a mode entered has to be held -- the same structure
    as minimum up-time in unit commitment, and just as weak in the relaxation;
  · semi-continuous charge rates: a unit is off, or at or above its turndown,
    never between;
  · tank inventory balances with finite capacity linking every slot to the next,
    which is what stops the horizon decoupling into T independent problems.

Yields depend on the MODE.  That dependence is the point: it is the thing a
fixed-yield planning LP cannot express, and it is why this family is not just
`production_planning` with binaries bolted on.

Unmet demand carries a shortfall variable at a penalty price, so the all-idle
schedule is always feasible and the model is bounded.  A refinery scheduler
writes the same term for the same reason.

  sched_m    6 units, 3 modes, 12 slots     972 x 924    432 binaries
  sched_l    8 units, 4 modes, 16 slots    2200 x 1968  1024 binaries

2. PROCESS OPTIMIZATION  (convex QP)  --  bench/generate_qp.py: process_rto
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
The steady-state real-time-optimization layer: above the regulatory controls,
below the planner.  Each unit has manipulated variables (severity, reflux, feed
rate, catalyst circulation) and controlled ones (a product property, a bed
temperature, a column loading), and around the current operating point the plant
is described by its GAIN MATRIX -- the linearised steady state a plant test
produces -- so

    y_u - G_u x_u = y_u^0

is a linear equality and the optimizer picks a new operating point subject to
it.  Three terms in the objective, each quadratic for a physical reason:

  · deviation of a controlled variable from target, squared, because being twice
    as far off spec is worse than twice as bad;
  · a move penalty on the manipulated variables, which is what stops the RTO
    handing the regulatory layer a step it cannot make;
  · utility consumption.  Compressor and pump power rise roughly with the square
    of throughput, so the energy term really is quadratic.

The Hessian is deliberately RANK DEFICIENT: about 40% of the controlled
variables sit in a spec band with no target, so they appear in the constraints
and not in Q at all.  That is the case a solver assuming a positive definite Q
gets wrong.  Utility headers couple every unit, so the model does not decompose
into `nunit` small ones.

  qrto_s      6 units,  5 MV,  4 CV      28 x   56      47 quadratic terms
  qrto_m     14 units,  8 MV,  6 CV      90 x  199     166 quadratic terms
  qrto_l    150 units, 12 MV, 10 CV    1510 x 3305    2701 quadratic terms

AGAINST OSQP -- 9 instances, OSQP at eps 1e-9 with polishing

  agreeing with OSQP .............. 9 / 9
  worst relative difference ....... 3.2e-08  (qrto_m_1)
  worst violation, IGAOS .......... 7.1e-13
  worst violation, OSQP ........... 1.1e-14
  total time ...................... IGAOS 0.12 s, OSQP 0.08 s

  Every instance takes the interior-point path and closes in 7 to 10 iterations;
  qrto_l is 3,305 columns and 2,701 quadratic terms in 40 ms.  OSQP is a
  first-order method, so a disagreement in the eighth digit is as likely to be
  the reference as the solver under test -- both objectives are printed by the
  harness so a reader can judge instead of taking a verdict.

  These are small and easy, and saying so is more useful than dressing them up.
  An RTO problem IS small: a refinery-wide one is thousands of variables, not
  millions, and it is solved every few minutes rather than once a month.  The
  hard part of process optimization is not the size.


WHAT THESE TWO FAMILIES STILL DO NOT COVER
------------------------------------------
The POOLING PROBLEM.  Blend quality times flow is bilinear, so tracking true
component qualities through an intermediate tank is nonconvex -- an MINLP, not a
MILP or a QP.  Everything above, and `refinery_blend` alongside it, uses the
linear volume-basis approximation, which is the standard PLANNING form and is
what an LP-based refinery planner actually solves.  It is not the true problem.

That is the one honest gap left in the process-optimization scope area, it is
the same gap docs/EXTENSION-NLP-MINLP.md describes, and no instance in this file
closes it.
